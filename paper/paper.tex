\begin{abstract}
A rhythm-game chart must fit its music and play well, yet the same
song admits many valid charts. Charting is design, so agreement with
the one official chart penalizes valid alternatives along with real
flaws. Freedom of note choice still leaves one requirement that no
alternative escapes: whatever notes a chart chooses must stand on the
song's musical time. Timing is the floor of musical fit. We introduce
ChartGenEval, a metric suite that measures this floor without
treating any note sequence as the answer: it takes from the official
chart only its authored timing map, never its note placements. Its
whole-chart grid-phase offset recovers injected 15--60\,ms shifts
with zero median error---shifts that leave every primary chart-only
output within 0.03 standard deviations of the human charts.

Above the floor, quality admits many answers, so ChartGenEval
replaces the single ranking with a score profile: six
dimensions---timing, note sequence, repetition and form, response to
music, distance from same-difficulty human charts, and playability
limits---are scored separately, with no overall total. Controlled corruptions show why:
rewriting toward common patterns lowers language-model perplexity by
37\%, and loop collapse raises self-similarity by 62\%. Ten frozen
corruption--measurement tests, selected on a 40-song development
panel, all survived held-out confirmation on 80 untouched song
groups, with all 32 scoped controls passing; one algebraically
coupled pair leaves nine nonredundant tests. Other proposed
components carry development evidence; criterion validity against
player judgments remains open. Applied to five public generators, the
suite reveals timing and difficulty-specific departures that a single
ranking would hide. We release the implementation, corruptions,
calibration, and per-chart records at \artifacturl.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Automatic chart generation for rhythm games---mapping audio to a
playable sequence of timed, typed notes---has progressed from
onset-detection pipelines to conditional sequence
models~\cite{donahue2017ddc,halina2021taikonation,takada2023genelive,
hanzen2025tcp}. Evaluation has not kept pace. A chart dictates what the
player must do and when; it answers to the music and to the player at
once. We organize evaluation around two questions. Does the chart
\emph{fit the music}---are its notes anchored to the beat, and does its
activity follow the music's rise and fall? And does it \emph{play
well}---does it offer learnable patterns and repetition with variation,
and are its demands matched to the requested difficulty? A chart is a designed
artifact, not an annotation: fit admits many answers, and how those
answers play is what separates them.

Existing methods answer useful but partial questions. The dominant
evaluation, placement F1 against the one official chart, quantifies
reconstruction: a valid alternative lowers overlap exactly as a flaw
does, and no comparison is possible when the song has no reference
chart. Optimizing agreement therefore rewards reproducing the official
answer and penalizes the departures that make charting a design problem.
Language-model (LM) likelihood quantifies model fit; distributional
and self-similarity statistics describe generated material; and player
or expert studies establish system-level experience. Each answers its
own question, and an aggregate of them hides its trade-offs.

Evaluation that leaves note choices free must still hold a floor.
However free the notes, they still answer to the song's musical
time: a chart whose notes drift off the beat fails before questions of
quality begin. Timing is the \emph{floor} of musical fit---a necessary
condition that any valid alternative must still meet. Our first move
is therefore to split the official chart's two roles: we take only its
authored timing map, never its note placements, so the musical-time
framework is shared while the note choices within it stay free. The
proposed chart-to-grid timing profile and whole-chart grid-phase
offset make this floor testable without a reference note sequence; the
offset search recovers injected 15--60\,ms shifts that every primary
chart-only measurement misses.

A chart that stands on the floor is not yet a good chart. Above the
floor, quality has degrees and admits many answers, so
\textbf{ChartGenEval} replaces the single ranking with a score
profile: six dimensions
organized around musical fit and play characteristics
(\figref{fig:overview}). Beyond timing, the suite asks whether
activity follows the music (two chart--audio response measures),
whether long-form structure returns without stagnating (ABAB
reciprocity and a stagnation--alienation proxy), and how far a chart
sits from human charts of the same course (a 32-dimensional
distance). These proposed components are combined
with established chart statistics---including note density, interval
and transition patterns, and 4-gram repetition---that we convert into
same-difficulty typicality scores or one-sided limit checks; the
playability limits are floors of their own. The suite reports all
dimensions separately and defines no universal total.

Controlled corruptions supply the sensitivity evidence. Starting from a human
chart, we add a construction-guaranteed flaw at three strengths and ask
whether its declared measurement worsens while scoped controls remain
invariant. The nine corruptions cover dense timing jitter, sparse timing
outliers, global timing shift, note-type shuffling, loop collapse,
common-pattern rewriting, density scaling, burst insertion, and bar
shuffling. Development experiments select and revise candidate
measurements. A separately frozen confirmation then evaluates ten declared
corruption--measurement tests on untouched songs. This two-stage design keeps
exploratory candidate evidence distinct from held-out confirmation.

Three development findings explain why the dimensions remain
separate. LM perplexity rewards rewriting toward common patterns;
self-similarity rewards loop collapse; and every primary chart-only
output misses a constant chart--audio shift. In the frozen
confirmation, all ten declared tests and all 32 scoped controls pass
(\secref{sec:dependency}).

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A multi-dimensional metric suite.} ChartGenEval defines
  proposed chart-to-grid, chart--audio, long-form structure, and
  human-distance measurements, and combines them with
  difficulty-conditioned adaptations of established chart statistics.
  Each output has a declared interpretive role and remains separate.
\item \textbf{Chart-specific operationalizations.} We define a
  whole-chart grid-phase offset, bar-level density--energy
  response, run-head onset support, ABAB reciprocity, a
  stagnation--alienation structural proxy, and a course-conditioned
  32-dimensional human-chart gap. The grid-phase offset is included in
  held-out confirmation; the other five currently carry development
  evidence.
\item \textbf{Controlled-corruption evidence.} Nine dose-controlled
  corruptions test target response and scoped invariance, yielding nine
  nonredundant held-out corruption--measurement tests.
  The same experiments identify sign reversals in perplexity and
  self-similarity and the global-shift blind spot of chart-only metrics.
\item \textbf{System-level use and reproducible materials.} Compatible
  components applied to five public generators localize timing,
  sequence, density, and difficulty-specific departures. We release the
  implementation, corruptions, calibration values, and per-chart
  records at \artifacturl.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Reference-chart agreement.} DDC and Gen\'eLive! report F-scores
for predicted placements, while TCP reports onset and onset-plus-type
F1 together with local pattern
recall~\cite{donahue2017ddc,takada2023genelive,hanzen2025tcp}. Their
tolerances and aggregation differ: DDC uses $\pm20$\,ms, Gen\'eLive!
uses $\pm50$\,ms, and TCP defines tolerances as fractions of a bar.
TaikoNation instead compares 23\,ms binary frames and pattern
spaces~\cite{halina2021taikonation}. These are reconstruction estimands and
inherit the single-reference limits described in \secref{sec:intro}.

\textbf{Unpaired and human-anchored evaluation.} DDC uses LM perplexity
and token accuracy as held-out model-fit measures, rather than as
generated-chart quality scores~\cite{donahue2017ddc}. Symbolic-music and
text-generation work contributes diversity, feature-distribution,
self-similarity, and set-level repetition
statistics~\cite{li2016diversity,zhu2018texygen,yang2020evaluation,dong2020muspy,
wu2020jazz}. Human studies answer another question. MuTE validates a
reference-aligned piano-arrangement measure against expert
ratings~\cite{gover2022mute}; Gen\'eLive! reports professional deployment
feedback~\cite{takada2023genelive}; and Mania Archetype combines
reference similarities with ratings from eight experienced
players~\cite{wang2024mania}. ChartGenEval supplies the automatic measurements and
corruption-sensitivity evidence that such human studies can build on.

\textbf{Metric stress tests and timing.} SCHmUBERT constructs
non-musical piano rolls that match framewise self-similarity
statistics~\cite{plasser2023schmubert}. Fr\'echet Music Distance is tested with
pitch and velocity perturbations, but remains a set-level embedding
distance~\cite{retkowski2025fmd}. STRUM reports reference-onset F1 after
a per-song global-offset search~\cite{opria2026strum}; ChartGenEval
reports the whole-chart grid-phase offset as a chart-level measurement
without using reference notes as targets. Our 6\,ms deadzone follows temporal discrimination
research~\cite{friberg1995jnd,london2012hearing}
(\secref{sec:timingmethod}).

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{ChartGenEval metric suite}\label{sec:suite}

ChartGenEval separates profile construction from corruption-sensitivity testing.
The suite first defines the construct, inputs, outputs, and interpretive
role of each measurement. Controlled corruptions then test the declared
responses. Table~\ref{tab:suite} identifies which measurements are
proposed here and which adapt established chart statistics, together
with the strongest evidence available in this study.

The proposed measurements target gaps left by individual chart
statistics. The \emph{whole-chart grid-phase offset} searches shifts from
$-250$ to $+250$\,ms against a fixed lattice of bar, beat, and
eighth-note anchors. To test whether activity follows the music's rise
and fall, the \emph{density--energy response} is the Spearman
correlation between hits per second and linear mel energy in authored
bar windows. It is unavailable with fewer than eight windows or when
the song's 90th--10th percentile energy range is below 5\% of its
median. The \emph{run-head onset support} is the fraction of
rhythmic-group heads whose maximum spectral flux within three mel frames
($\approx\pm35$\,ms) reaches the song's 60th-percentile threshold. A
new group begins after an inter-onset interval of at least 0.25\,s,
preventing dense runs from dominating the denominator.

For long-form structure, \emph{reciprocity} divides the chart into
two-bar phrases on sixteenth-note slots. A tolerant,
chance-corrected rhythm similarity is multiplied by chance-corrected
color agreement. If $S(i,j)$ denotes this product with one-slot
tolerance, the score is the phrase mean of
$S(i,i+2)[1-S(i,i+1)]$: material returns after the intervening phrase
without rewarding adjacent copy-paste repetition.

The \emph{stagnation--alienation proxy} operates on active-bar rhythm
and color fingerprints. A bar is stagnant when it is the fourth or
later member of a consecutive similarity-$\geq.8$ run, or when every
eligible 16-note color window is exactly periodic with period at most
four. A bar is alienated when no bar with similarity at least .75
occurs within 32 bars in either direction. The raw output is the
fraction of active bars in the union of these sets. The operational
name is deliberate: the measurement describes chart structure, and
perceived boredom remains untested. Finally, the \emph{human-chart
gap} represents each chart with 32 interval, note-type, density, and
variety features. Features are standardized within course; the raw gap
is the mean Euclidean distance to the 20 nearest training charts of
that course. Its bounded display score is $\exp(-g/a)$, where $g$ is
the raw gap and $a$ is the course's 90th percentile leave-one-out gap.
The representation is chart-specific; its entropy, bigram, and
nearest-neighbor primitives are established statistics.

\subsection{Data and timing references}\label{sec:data}

We use taiko-style chart files and associated audio gathered from
community resources available online. The chart files transcribe the
official difficulty courses; we use them as human reference charts.
Because the underlying charts and audio are potentially copyrighted
content, the corpus itself is not distributed: the released artifact
carries the derived calibration statistics and per-chart records, and
the analysis fixes one corpus snapshot whose hash is pinned by the
freeze manifest. Source rows are
canonicalized into song groups by a stable group identifier and audio
SHA-256, with inconsistent mappings rejected. The training split has
924 source rows, 813 canonical song groups, and 3{,}880 course charts;
its difficulty-course charts are used to fit the calibration and the
LM. The test split
has 140 source rows that canonicalize to 120 stored-order song groups.
The first 40 canonical groups, comprising 170 human charts across the
available courses, form the development panel used for metric and corruption
design, figures, and system profiles. The remaining groups at indices
$[40,120)$ formed the 80-song confirmation panel. They were held
untouched until the protocol, implementation, calibration, runtime, and
analysis contract described below had been frozen.

The five development-only proposed components follow a specification
written after their selection and revision, so their analysis remains
exploratory. The released records bind every result to its exact
inputs by hash.

Timing requires an external song-timing reference. The primary grid
comes from the matched official chart file's authored per-bar
timestamps, meter, and tempo metadata. Its note times and note types
are never used as targets: the official file supplies the timing map,
not a single correct note sequence. This retains the map's precise
authored bar structure while allowing generated charts to make
different valid note choices within the same musical-time framework.
We never use the grid declared by the generated chart, because that
would let the generator define its own reference.

The general profile can use audio-estimated downbeats when an
authored timing map is unavailable. On 39 of the 40 development
songs, the estimated beat period is within 2\% of the metadata-based
period; the remaining song differs by 2.39\%. The two sources can
still disagree on the first beat, so the grid-phase offset search
below is designed to tolerate that ambiguity. Absolute-offset
reporting uses the authored grid because audio must infer bar phase.
The frozen confirmation used only the authored per-course timing map;
if it is missing, the affected timing measurements are recorded as
unavailable rather than replaced by the audio-estimated grid.

\subsection{Measurement roles and difficulty calibration}\label{sec:layers}

Each chart is a sequence of timed, typed notes. We organize the raw
measurements around the six question groups in \figref{fig:overview},
which divide between the two requirements of \secref{sec:intro}.
Musical fit is agreement between what is heard and what is played, so
timing and response to music require the song. The other four groups do
not require audio and describe play characteristics; the proposed
structure measurements additionally require a metrical grid. The adapted chart-side primitives are a
smoothed trigram LM, unseen-transition rate, note rate, local density,
short-interval load, entropy, and unique or repeated 4-grams. We retain
LM loss as a model-fit description. Read as mean per-note information
content, the same quantity is called \emph{pattern-IC}; its
same-difficulty band score serves only as a secondary analysis. The released name
\texttt{transition\_validity\_score} is interpreted more narrowly as
transition familiarity: a trigram unseen in the training corpus is not
necessarily invalid. The proposed structure, audio-response, timing,
and human-gap measurements are defined in \secref{sec:suite}.

We turn each selected feature into a
\emph{same-difficulty band score}. For every course and selected
feature, let $l$ and $u$ be the 10th and 90th percentiles of the training
charts and let $h=\max((u-l)/2,10^{-9})$ be the protected half-width.
The distance is symmetric at the two edges:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
d(x)&=
\begin{cases}
(l-x)/h, & x<l,\\
0,       & l\leq x\leq u,\\
(x-u)/h, & x>u,
\end{cases}\\
s(x)&=\frac{1}{1+d(x)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:band}
\end{equation}
Thus a chart scores 1 inside the band, and equal departures below and
above it receive equal scores. We also retain the signed distance so
that large departures do not collapse to the same near-zero score.

This score measures how unusual a chart is for the requested
difficulty; it does not by itself say whether the chart is good. Some
measurements have a declared undesirable direction, such as excess
load beyond a course-relative limit; these use separately named
one-sided scores. Transition familiarity is also one-sided relative to
the training corpus, without implying semantic invalidity. Family
measurements without a declared calibration remain raw descriptions.
Entropy, density, and repetition have no universal direction: each can
be too low or too high. Controlled corruptions support only
one implication: known damage tends to move these values away from the
human band. An unusual chart that remains playable and follows the
music may still be creative (\secref{sec:discussion}).

We use each result according to its declared role and evidence tier.
Playability limits remain separate floor checks. On human charts in the
development panel, 88\%, 89\%, and 97\% score at least 0.99 on the
note-rate, short-interval, and density-spike checks, respectively.
Values such as estimated global shift remain descriptive. The public
interface returns named measurements and checks separately.

\subsection{Measuring timing against the song}\label{sec:timingmethod}

We use 6\,ms as the deadzone, following the published just-noticeable
difference for short intervals~\cite{friberg1995jnd}. This result
motivates a tolerance for grid alignment; it is not a validated gameplay
threshold for this corpus. Larger errors are reported in three ranges:
above 6, 12, and 18\,ms. We also compare consecutive note
intervals using a tolerance of
$\max(6\,\mathrm{ms},2.5\%\times\mathrm{IOI})$. Every note is counted
as within tolerance, above one of the three ranges, or unmatched.
Unmatched generated notes remain in the denominator, so added off-grid
notes lower the within-tolerance rate. The measure has no reference-note
recall term: deleting unmatched generated notes can raise the rate. We
therefore interpret it together with note count and same-difficulty
density. We summarize each chart
first, then report medians and tails across charts.

Nearest-grid matching can miss a constant shift (\figref{fig:timing}).
On a dense grid, each shifted note may simply be paired with another
nearby grid point. We therefore estimate one offset for the whole chart.
For every candidate between $-250$ and $+250$\,ms, we measure how many
notes align with fixed subdivisions inside the reference bars, then
select the offset with the strongest agreement. The authored grid takes
these bars from the official chart's timing map, not its note sequence;
the audio-estimated grid infers them from audio downbeats. Combining bar,
beat, and eighth-note subdivisions resolves most periodic ties; the
smallest-magnitude offset breaks any remaining tie.
In the formal protocol, both timing summaries require one of these external grids. If neither an
authored map nor an audio-estimated grid is available, timing is reported
as unavailable. BPM alone supplies a period but no phase, so it cannot
serve as a timing reference.

The development response matrix is exploratory. It reports standardized
maximum-dose changes and the per-chart net direction---the fraction
moving up minus the fraction moving down relative to each chart's intact
version---over all 170 development charts. These charts are not treated
as independent inferential units, and the matrix carries no chance band
or cell-level significance claim. Raw features and their derived scores
are evaluated on the same variants. Equivalent quantities are not
counted as independent agreement, and note-count associations are
reported separately. For the development C5 check, one LM chooses the
common-pattern rewrites and another scores them; the perplexity reversal
remains.

\textbf{Frozen confirmation.} The ten primary tests in
\tabref{tab:probes} are evaluated on the untouched 80-song panel. Every
nonzero corruption cell and every control condition uses the five fixed base seeds
1103, 2207, 3301, 4409, and 5519. Replicates are first averaged within
each chart and dose. After orienting every measurement so that larger is
better, the first statistic is the within-chart Spearman correlation
between the four frozen severities (intact plus three doses) and the
measurement; an all-constant response is defined as zero sensitivity.
The second statistic is the oriented maximum-dose value minus the
test's fixed scoped sham. A course is complete only when its intact
value, all target doses and replicates, sham, and required controls are
available. A song enters a test with at least one complete course;
no-op or missing cells remain in the exclusion ledger. Complete courses
are averaged within song, so songs---not
charts or courses---are the inferential units for both statistics.

The scoped controls test invariance within declared families. Identity
re-evaluation is exact for every retained measurement. A joint
+1-second shift of notes, authored grid, and duration is invariant for
timing, density, grammar, and structure. A Don--Ka color bijection is a
sham only for timing and density; it is not assumed neutral for grammar
or form. The 60\,ms C2 translation is the sham for chart-only density,
grammar, and structure. A required control failure makes the affected
test inconclusive rather than being averaged away. Metric-specific
equivalence tolerances and the timestamp rounding rules are fixed in
the released contract. Chart-only interval arithmetic uses an
integer-microsecond clock, three orders of magnitude finer than the
smallest reported tolerance, which makes interval tokens and half-open
density windows invariant to a translated time origin.

Point estimates average song-level values. We report nominal 95\%
percentile intervals from 10{,}000 song-cluster bootstrap draws with seed
20260712. The confirmation gate uses Bonferroni-adjusted 99.5\%
intervals for the ten primary tests. A test \texttt{SURVIVES} only with
at least 72 of 80 evaluable songs, passing controls, and adjusted upper
bounds below zero for both statistics. After the same gates pass, an
adjusted lower bound at or above zero for either statistic yields
\texttt{REFUTED}; all other outcomes are \texttt{INCONCLUSIVE}. The frozen C5
combination rule requires both co-primary tests to survive;
\secref{sec:dependency} shows they measure one effective axis.
Provenance, panel, or hash failure yields \texttt{INVALID}.

Confirmation opened only after the complete protocol---dataset
revision, corruptions and doses, primary measurements, controls,
thresholds, calibration, and source code---was frozen under a hash
manifest; any mismatch aborts the run before a verdict is produced.
The C5 LM is fixed to the full stored-order training split, trigram
order 3, and add-$\alpha=0.05$.

\subsection{Evaluated systems}\label{sec:systems}

We evaluate five public generators spanning three note formats: Taiko
note types, timing-only onset streams, and osu! position-based objects.
Two systems produce Taiko note types: Mapperatorinator
v32~\cite{mapperatorinator}, a community-developed, MIT-licensed
framework for multi-gamemode osu! beatmap generation evaluated here in
taiko mode, and the FDG-published TaikoNation
system~\cite{halina2021taikonation}, reproduced from the authors'
public research repository\footnote{The TaikoNation repository does not
state a software license. The released artifact contains provenance and
derived metric records, but no TaikoNation code, checkpoints, input
assets, or generated chart data.}. Three systems enter only the timing
and music-response measurements: Dance Dance Convolution's onset
pipeline (ddconset)~\cite{donahue2017ddc}, the public timing-only
configuration of Gen\'eLive!~\cite{takada2023genelive}, and
AutoOsu~\cite{autoosu}, an osu! difficulty-targeted generator. All
systems chart the same 40 development songs from audio in
their public inference configurations. We did not train or fine-tune
these systems on this corpus, although overlap between these commercial songs and the
systems' own training data cannot be excluded
(\secref{sec:discussion}). Course mappings are explicit in the released
configuration. Mapperatorinator uses the corresponding Taiko course.
Gen\'eLive's Beginner, Easy, Medium, Hard, and Challenge settings map to
Easy, Normal, Hard, Oni, and Ura; when a song has no Ura chart,
Challenge uses Oni as its reference course. Single-setting systems
use the declared Oni reference. Format conversion into the evaluation
event stream is shared within each note format
(\secref{sec:systemresults}). Human
charts of the same songs provide the reference row. Systems that
condition on difficulty produce one chart per available course (170 or
200 charts); the others produce one per song (40). These profiles are
descriptive development analyses and are not part of the frozen
confirmation gate.

\subsection{The C5 dependency leaves one effective axis}
\label{sec:dependency}

The formal C5 all-pairs rule was mechanically satisfied because both
named tests worsened under the common-pattern rewrite. They cannot be
interpreted as corroborating measurements: repeated 4-gram rate is
exactly one minus unique 4-gram rate, and their mirrored symmetric-band
scores are identical up to floating-point roundoff. C5 therefore
establishes sensitivity of one effective 4-gram
repetition/uniqueness test.

The pattern-IC band score remained a secondary, direction-free analysis: its
maximum-dose change was $+0.021$ with a nominal 95\% interval
$[-0.034,0.077]$, and its dose statistic likewise crossed zero
($-0.106$, $[-0.225,0.016]$). Raw pattern NLL fell from 2.087 to
1.715 (maximum-dose change $-0.373$, 95\% CI
$[-0.399,-0.346]$). The LM therefore assigned greater likelihood to
the rewritten charts, without establishing a pattern improvement. The
supported cross-axis result is narrower: the effective 4-gram test
detects the rewrite that pattern-IC and LM likelihood do not label as
damage. Testing independent complementarity requires a genuinely
non-equivalent preregistered structure measurement.

\subsection{Behavior of the proposed components}
\label{sec:newmetricresults}

The development-only components in \tabref{tab:suite} were assessed as
candidate measurements, without held-out confidence claims. Their
pooled Spearman responses across intact charts and three doses support
distinct operational roles. Reciprocity fell
under loop collapse and bar shuffling
($\rho=-.612$ and $-.506$) while remaining nearly unchanged under
timing jitter and global translation ($-.086$ and $-.003$). The
stagnation--alienation proxy showed a strong response to loop collapse
($\rho=-.924$ after orienting larger as better), yet was effectively
unchanged by timing jitter and global translation ($-.007$ and
$+.010$). These results support structural sensitivity in development;
they do not establish perceived call-and-response or boredom.

The two audio-response measures capture different scales. Run-head
onset support decreased under a global shift ($\rho=-.559$), whereas
bar-level density--energy response was stable under uniform density
scaling ($\rho=+.005$) and fell under bar shuffling ($\rho=-.386$).
Its burst-insertion response was weak ($\rho=-.092$), so this measure should be read specifically as bar-scale
covariation rather than a general audio-fit score. The course-specific
human-chart gap is likewise a joint-distribution description: it can
flag combinations unlike the training cloud, but does not determine
whether an unusual chart is creative or poor. At maximum strength, its
course-conditioned score has net directions from $-.32$ to $-.88$
under C3--C8. Under global translation, its median change is zero at
every dose and its maximum-dose net direction is $-.02$. The C5
response is aggregate and dose-specific: the score worsens at maximum
dose but not at the two lower doses, and course-level directions differ.
These development
results motivated inclusion in the released suite while retaining the
evidence boundary shown in \tabref{tab:suite}. A released development
table records the response of each of the five components to all nine
corruptions.

\subsection{Isolated proxies can reward damage}
\label{sec:reductio}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figs/figA_complementarity.pdf}
\caption{Two cases in which one measurement misses a flaw and another
detects it. Bars show the fraction of charts moving up minus the
fraction moving down relative to the intact chart; light to dark marks
the three corruption strengths on the development panel (descriptive,
not independent-chart confidence statements). \textbf{Left:}
common-pattern rewriting lowers LM loss and raises the pattern-IC band
score, while the 4-gram repetition/uniqueness axis moves the other way
(its two algebraically equivalent frozen names are plotted once).
\textbf{Right:} sparse $\pm$60\,ms timing errors leave the mean timing
error near zero and are mostly re-paired by the nearest grid, while the
note-sequence LM responds to the changed intervals.}
\label{fig:complementarity}
\end{figure*}

Two borrowed metrics reverse sign under damage
(\figref{fig:complementarity}, left). \emph{Perplexity rewards common
patterns}: perplexity is the exponential of a chart's mean per-note
trigram loss. Human development charts average 9.44; rewriting them toward the
LM's most common patterns lowers perplexity by 37\% (9.44 $\to$ 5.98). Dance
Dance Convolution reports perplexity as model fit on held-out human
charts~\cite{donahue2017ddc}; transplanted to generated output as a
reference-free quality reading, the usual lower-is-better interpretation
prefers the damaged chart. This is a chart-level example of the
likelihood/quality dissociation~\cite{theis2016note}. The reversal
remains when one LM creates the rewrite and another scores it
(9.44 $\to$ 6.04, $-$36\%). The pattern-IC band score moves toward its
human band and increases across the three doses. Calibration therefore
does not repair C5 on its own: it measures typicality on this axis, and
the rewritten chart can become more typical while losing variety. The
effective 4-gram axis (\secref{sec:dependency}) falls from 0.99 to 0.87
in development.
\emph{Structureness
rewards collapse}: replacing charts with their own dominant 4-gram loop
raises self-similarity structureness~\cite{wu2020jazz} by 62\% (0.0848
$\to$ 0.1370) and set-level Self-BLEU~\cite{zhu2018texygen} from 0.845
to 0.898---copy-paste output is more self-similar by construction---while
the 4-gram repetition/uniqueness score falls from 0.99 to 0.83.

Two observational results complete the case. \emph{Direction
instability}: against the two publicly available taiko generators of
\secref{sec:systemresults}, whose outputs lie on the high-entropy side of the human corpus,
perplexity separates human from generated charts in the expected
direction (AUC 0.80)---yet C5 proves the
same reading inverts on the low-entropy side. A signed quality metric whose sign
depends on which side of the human distribution the system sits cannot
be used for ranking; the same-difficulty band score treats both sides
symmetrically as deviations from typicality. C5 shows why that score
must be read together with the 4-gram repetition/uniqueness
axis. \emph{Length confounds}: on the same pool, type--token
ratio correlates with note count at $|\rho|=0.85$ and distinct-2 at
$0.56$, against at most $0.28$ for the band scores.

\subsection{External time reveals global misalignment}
\label{sec:anchorresults}

This is where the floor gives way: a chart can have plausible
density, patterns, and note types---the primary chart-only readings---while
being late relative to the music. C2 shifts every note by the same
15, 30, or 60\,ms. On the development panel, no primary
chart-only output changes by more than 0.03 human standard deviations. This is expected: a measurement
that reads only intervals and note types is unchanged when every time
is translated by the same amount.

Nearest-grid matching also misses the shift because the dense grid pairs
each note with another nearby point. The grid-phase offset search instead
recovers exact median shifts of 15.00, 30.00, and 60.00\,ms from both
reference sources. For every source and shift, at least 169 of 170
charts are within 3\,ms of the injected value. The run-head
onset-support measurement also detects the shift at note onsets (net
direction $-0.74$ to $-0.88$ across strengths) while remaining
unchanged when note times are preserved. Under dense timing
jitter (C1), the fraction of notes within 6\,ms falls in every chart at
every strength. At $\pm$10\,ms, the measured median outside the
tolerance is 0.40, matching $P(|U(-10,10)|>6)=0.4$.

\subsection{Different measurements catch different flaws}
\label{sec:complementarity}

Three corruptions show why the results must remain separate
(\figref{fig:complementarity}, \figref{fig:responsemap}):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C2 global shift} is invisible to every primary chart-only
  measurement and to nearest-grid matching. The grid-phase offset search and
  the run-head onset support detect it.
\item \textbf{C1s sparse timing errors} are mostly re-paired by the
  nearest grid, so the cross-chart median of mean timing error stays at
  zero. The
  note-sequence LM responds strongly because moving one note changes
  the two adjacent intervals. Density remains essentially unchanged, and form
  responds only weakly.
\item \textbf{C5 common-pattern rewrite} lowers perplexity and raises
  pattern-IC band fit in development; the confirmatory pattern-IC interval
  crosses zero and raw NLL still falls. The effective 4-gram
  repetition/uniqueness test worsens, giving cross-axis coverage.
\end{itemize}

Each group misses a flaw that another group detects. Averaging them
would require trade-off weights that the evidence does not provide.

In the development system profiles the two questions dissociate.
Figures~\ref{fig:tiers}--\ref{fig:profiles} show two ways in which a
single number would average away important differences.

\textbf{Similar timing fractions can have different causes.} In
\figref{fig:tiers}, the near-unity human row is a consistency check on
using the authored bar metadata as the reference, not a model result.
The two
Taiko systems place nearly the same median fraction of notes within
6\,ms (0.59 and 0.58). Their remaining timing behavior differs.
Mapperatorinator places 10\% of adjacent intervals outside the relative
tolerance, compared with 41\% for TaikoNation. The grid-phase offset
search finds median offsets of 26 and 55\,ms, while nearest-grid matching
reports less than 1\,ms mean error beyond the 6\,ms deadzone for both.
TaikoNation also shows almost no
relation between chart activity and audio energy (correlation
$-0.003$), and its notes fall on average-energy frames. The DDC onset
system gives a 2.5\,ms offset on the same audio, serving as a control
against a shared decoding shift. AutoOsu, which shares
Mapperatorinator's osu! conversion path, reads a 19\,ms median offset;
the differing values rule out a shared conversion constant as the sole
explanation (\secref{sec:systems}).

\textbf{The requested course changes the reading.} Mapperatorinator's
generated-to-human note-rate ratio falls from 2.15--2.35 on Easy and
Normal to 1.31 on Oni and 1.07 on Ura
(\figref{fig:difficulty}). Its note-rate, short-interval, and
local-pattern scores are below 0.5 on Easy through Hard and near 1 on
Oni and Ura. Timing does not follow the same trend: median fractions
within 6\,ms are 0.56, 0.60, 0.59, 0.57, and 0.62. The supported claim
is therefore a difficulty mismatch, not a general improvement
at high difficulty. TaikoNation exposes only one setting; at that
setting, note rate and short-interval load fit the human range, while
55\% of its trigrams are unseen in the course-conditioned training LM
and its repeated-4-gram rate is
0.004. These are different weaknesses, not a basis for one overall
ranking.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

ChartGenEval contributes at the metric-suite level: each output has a
declared construct, interpretive role, and evidence tier, and the
dimensions remain separate because a timing offset, an unfamiliar
transition, a density mismatch, and structural stagnation describe
different concerns. The frozen confirmation carries the held-out
evidence (\secref{sec:sensitivity}); the other proposed components
retain development-only status.

\textbf{Atypicality is a warning, not a quality verdict.} The floor is
necessary, not sufficient: above it, quality admits many answers.
Controlled corruptions show that known damage tends to move scores
outside the human band. It does not prove that every unusual chart is bad. If a chart
remains playable, follows the music, and retains learnable patterns, an
unusual value may represent a creative choice. If those checks fail as
well, the same value supports a failure reading. Human judgments are
needed to distinguish these cases. The distance scale helps put large
departures in context: the development human maximum is 4.8 half-range
widths on the sequence axis, while the Mapperatorinator and TaikoNation
medians are 2.1 and 12, respectively. In this study, the course mismatch in
\secref{sec:systemresults} is the only worked interpretation of these
combined signals.

\textbf{Beyond one game.} Timing and music-response measurements read
only note times, so we applied them unchanged to three note formats.
Sequence, form, and distance measurements also need a tokenizer for
the target game and human charts for calibration. Lane, position, or
direction tokens can replace Taiko note types. The controlled corruptions
operate on generic timed, typed events, so the same
design can test which declared responses transfer to another game.
Position-based games still
need additional checks for movement cost, hand allocation, and
rotation; the present measurements do not cover those demands.

\textbf{Validity scope.} Controlled corruptions establish sensitivity to
the injected flaws, not criterion validity against player or expert
judgments. The
nine corruptions do not exhaust chart quality: pairing a chart with the wrong
song, deleting musically important notes, and deliberately placing
notes between grid points remain untested. The reported directions are
population results, and individual charts may move toward a human band
after a corruption. The five development-only components require an untouched
confirmation or player study before stronger quality claims. The frozen
C5 contract also treated repetition and surface variety as separate
co-primary measurements. Their algebraic equivalence leaves one
effective test; future confirmation should use a non-equivalent
structure measurement.

\textbf{External validity.} One game corpus of unstated genre breadth
defines the human ranges. Systems with other note formats are evaluated
only on timing and music response, and every system is run in its public
configuration at one seed. Possible overlap between development songs
and system training corpora was not controlled. Per-format conversion
offsets are bounded by the shared-path comparison of
\secref{sec:systemresults} but cannot be fully excluded. The system profiles are therefore
comparative descriptions rather than a leaderboard.

\textbf{Measurement and inference.} Timing and sequence responses
overlap because sequence tokens include note intervals
(\figref{fig:responsemap}). Timing clean rate penalizes extra off-grid
notes but has no reference recall term, so deletion can improve it; the
measurement is unavailable without an external grid. Formal
confirmation uses the authored grid, while the audio-estimated grid has
development evidence only.
Courses are averaged within song, leaving songs as the inferential unit;
possible author-level dependence across songs is not modeled.
Deterministic five-seed replicates are averaged within chart and never
counted as additional samples.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced ChartGenEval, a six-dimensional metric suite for
rhythm-game charts built on a floor-first reading of chart quality.
Timing is the floor of musical fit, and the suite measures it using
only the official chart's authored timing map, never its note
placements. Above the floor, proposed components characterize
response to music, long-form structure, and distance from human
charts; difficulty-conditioned adaptations of established chart
statistics supply the remaining sequence, repetition, density, and
limit readings. Raw descriptions, two-sided typicality, and
one-sided checks retain distinct roles, with no overall score.

Controlled corruptions support nine nonredundant held-out tests; the
remaining proposed components
currently have development evidence. The same experiments explain the
suite's separation:
perplexity can reward common-pattern rewriting, self-similarity can
reward loop collapse, and the primary chart-only outputs do not detect a global
shift from the music. The whole-chart grid-phase offset closes that last
blind spot without treating one reference note sequence as the answer.
These findings establish corruption sensitivity within the present
corpus and design.

Applied to public generators, separate outputs expose timing,
sequence, density, and course-specific weaknesses that a single ranking
would hide. We release the implementation and evidence at
\artifacturl. Future chart metrics can enter the suite by declaring the
construct and role they serve, then demonstrating the corresponding
response under a targeted flaw and scoped invariance controls.

The study uses the community-transcribed charts and associated audio
